Four Platforms, One Specification

The same lead-intake system, built four times, on Zapier, Make, n8n and GoHighLevel, for the same client by the same person, instrumented identically, and put through the same five failure probes as far as each platform allowed. This page reports what came out. Its argument is that the meter matters more than the price, and the right platform depends on where the work happens.

Zapier · Z1 Make · M1 n8n · N1 GoHighLevel · G1 16 capture fields · 5 failure probes

The rule that governs this page

Every number here is either measured during a build, or it is a formula with its inputs named. There is no third category.

This matters more here than anywhere else, because a comparison page is exactly where invented numbers are conventional and expected. The genre is dominated by affiliate listicles with confident tables, so a comparison built from first-hand builds is hard to fake and worth publishing. That advantage evaporates the moment one cell is estimated and rendered as though it were observed.

So: placeholders render as placeholders. Where a build hasn't run, the cell shows an em-dash and the page says so. And prices are quoted with a date, or not at all: third-party sources agree closely on billing models and contradict each other on prices. The models are stable enough to build an argument on; the prices are not. The primary currency on this page is therefore meter units, with dollars derived at publication time from each vendor's own pricing page.

The short version, in my words.
  • I built the same lead-routing job on four platforms and broke each one on purpose, so you can see how they bill, how they fail and how hard they are to leave. The client is fictional and the data is synthetic.
  • The same guard-halted lead cost 1 Zapier task, 4 Make credits, 1 n8n execution or 2 GoHighLevel premium items, so plan prices alone don't compare the platforms.
  • Where the downstream-failure test ran, a failed step did not show as a failed run unless an alert or check had been added.
  • On the plans I used, only n8n and Make gave me an exportable file of the build: a 59,260-byte workflow JSON and a 28,052-byte blueprint. Zapier offers JSON export only on Team and Enterprise plans, and GoHighLevel moves automations as snapshots and share links, not files.
Status (published 13 October 2026). All four builds have run and been captured. Cells show a measured value, a derived value with its inputs named, or an em-dash not measured where nothing was measured. Where a probe or field did not apply to the build as built, it says n/a. No spine is claimed to satisfy all eight requirements: SMS and enrichment are absent from Zapier and GoHighLevel, the GoHighLevel build ran on a trial account, and the Zapier parent never ran live. Those deviations are listed in §2 and §10, not buried.

1 · The scenario, specified onceBefore any platform is named, so a reader can check that four implementations were held to one specification.

The client

Laneframe is a transport management platform for small freight brokerages and asset-light 3PLs. Thirty-one people. Three self-serve tiers at $149, $279 and $449 a month, so a won deal is worth roughly $3,300 a year. The motion is self-serve trial plus inbound demo request: about 40 trial signups and 25 demo requests a week, handled by two SDRs and one account executive, with the founder taking anything above 40 trucks. The average deal closes in 19 days or dies.

Laneframe is a composite client, not a real one: a specification written to a real sales motion so the system could be built against something with actual constraints. The builds are real and the failure conditions were induced deliberately. The company is the fiction; nothing else is.

The problem, in the client's words

Leads arrive from five places in four shapes and get worked by three people with different jobs. The founder should see the 40-truck brokerage whose owner filled in the demo form. The SDRs should see everything else worth a phone call, quickly. Nobody should see the student researching a dissertation, and nobody should call the same person twice because they started a trial on Tuesday and asked for a demo on Thursday.

The input contract

This section is the specification as written before the builds; §2 reports where each build departed from it. All four implementations accept the same five sources in the same four shapes. Any platform that needs the payload pre-normalised before it arrives has failed part of the test, and that fact is recorded rather than worked around.

SourceShape
Website demo formJSON, full_name single field
In-product trial signupJSON, first_name / last_name, plus product-usage fields
Paid-search landing pageJSON, nested: answers inside an array of question objects
Call-tracking toolJSON, phone-primary, often no email
Forwarded referral emailUnstructured prose, human-written, no schema at all

Required behaviour

  1. Normalise all five shapes to one contact record: name, email, phone in E.164, company, company domain, fleet size, role, source.
  2. Guard the input. Missing usable email or missing company halts and routes to human review. Nothing falls through to a default score.
  3. Enrich on company domain. Free-mail domains are flagged, not enriched.
  4. Score for fit on fleet size, role, industry, and whether the lead is product-qualified (already in the trial) or marketing-qualified (filled in a form).
  5. Deduplicate against existing contacts on normalised email and company domain. A returning lead routes to re-engagement, not to the bin.
  6. Route three ways: founder (deal, instant SMS, Slack alert), SDR (deal, round-robin, task due within the hour, acknowledgement email), self-serve (nurture tag, no human alerted).
  7. Log every lead, including rejects and duplicates.
  8. Respond inside 60 seconds on the founder and SDR paths.
Definition of done. A build counts as complete when it satisfies all eight requirements, survives all five failure probes, and has all sixteen capture fields recorded. A build that satisfies seven of eight is reported as satisfying seven of eight.

2.1 · Zapier (Z1)

Architecture. Three Zaps, not two: a parent of 31 steps and two Sub-Zap children (4 and 5 steps), 40 steps in all. The split was forced by Zapier's 32-step ceiling per Zap. Formatter for normalisation, Filter as the input guard, Code by Zapier for scoring, HubSpot lookups for dedupe.

Finding. The step ceiling changed the finished shape of the build. Twilio SMS never delivered (caller-ID and carrier filtering) and was dropped. The enrichment step errored with "Invalid API key" and was deleted to stay inside the step budget. That reflects the ceiling, not how easy the integrations were. Zapier surfaced no retry or backoff setting in any step editor and none was built; retry was never probed, because probe 3 had nothing left to fail.

Also measured. A Sub-Zap run billed +1 task against a documented 2; unresolved. HubSpot's dedupe is read-then-branch, and the race was not tested. The parent Zap never ran live, so the success-path meter and time to first touch are not measured and appear as em-dashes below.

2.2 · Make (M1)

Architecture. One scenario of 47 modules. Webhook, Parse JSON, Set Variables for normalisation, Data Store lookups then a write for dedupe, a filter before any paid step, a Router, and per-module error handlers that raise a Slack alert and then Resume.

What was observed. The error handlers work: probes 3a and 3b were caught and alerted, and the top-level status still read SUCCESS. No retry or backoff was exercised or observed; the SMS module fired once and the run billed 19 operations. Repeat exists on the platform and was not tried, so "genuine exponential backoff" is a platform capability this page does not claim to have seen. Make also rejects a bad connection at save time, which is why probe 3c could not be induced.

And it cuts the other way. An unhandled error silently deactivated the scenario and queued incoming webhooks behind it. Build friction recorded: a wrong Gmail connection, a Data Store .data. prefix, and the auto-deactivate and queue drain. The free plan caps the account at 1,000 operations a month and two active scenarios. On a per-module meter, every module of failure handling is a module you pay for; see §4.4.

2.3 · n8n (N1)

Architecture. One workflow of 37 nodes, self-hosted and live. Webhook with an immediate Respond node, a JavaScript Code node for normalisation, a Postgres ON CONFLICT claim for dedupe (a single statement, atomic by construction), a Switch for routing, a Merge to write one log row, and a global Error Trigger workflow behind it.

What was measured. Every run, whichever route, was one execution; the guard-halt (4 of 37 nodes) and the full founder route cost the same. The workflow export is 59,260 bytes. The VPS is a real invoice: $5.35 a month including VAT, backup included (period 2026-08-02 to 2026-09-01), attributed 100% to N1 so far. Tool-active build time was about 28 h 44 m over 5 calendar days, by the gap-sum method.

Failure behaviour, before and after. Probe 3a was a silent success before a fix: the run Succeeded, no alert fired, and the lead was logged as routed. After the added assert it throws and alerts. 3b hung for 2 min 20.5 s; 3c failed in 1.91 s with a 401. Retry On Fail was off on every node tested, so no retry was observed. The native setting is fixed-interval with a maximum of 5 tries (a configuration fact, not exercised), so true backoff would be hand-built. And the cost of the licence is not $0: it is the invoice above plus the attention.

2.4 · GoHighLevel (G1)

Architecture. About 61 actions over four workflows on a trial account. Native triggers, If/Else bands for scoring, a stamped custom field as the dedupe guard, round-robin assignment, and an email hold for out-of-hours leads. SMS was removed (A2P 10DLC unregistered, and the cost). G1·03 is therefore an email missed-call follow-up with zero enrollments; it was never run.

Where the platform did not meet the specification. The nested paid-search answers[] array was not unwrapped, so flat payloads were used: the input contract was not met for the nested shape, and that is recorded here as a failed part of the test. Runs were contactless until an explicit Create/Update Contact action was added. The dedupe failed until a stamp step was added. There is no code step and no text-parsing primitive, so a forwarded prose referral cannot become a contact record inside the platform. A prose referral payload (from, subject, body; no email key) was sent on 5 Oct 2026: the Create/Update Contact step errored (no value for any mapped field), the input guard's no-email branch executed, the review-inbox notification fired, and the tag and telemetry steps were skipped for lack of a contact. Measured: it halted for human review and did not reach scoring.

Failure behaviour. In probe 3 a failed Webhook action ran once and ended: no retry, no alert, and no backfill in 16 minutes. The failure stayed in the child workflow while the parent reported the handoff as executed.

What the comparison cannot say. The claims that GoHighLevel is the fastest to first touch, has the fewest moving parts, or has a missed-call text-back with no equivalent elsewhere are argued, not demonstrated: the text-back was never run, and the only first-touch measurement is an after-hours email (§6, finding 9). The data model is the platform's, and there is no per-workflow export.

Why this client size: sub-$500-a-month product, self-serve trial plus inbound demos, about thirty people. Without it a technical reader correctly asks why not HubSpot. Laneframe is also the client in §7's first row, and the matrix there says plainly what the data does and does not support.

The two observations to draw out explicitly

Unit counts across platforms are meaningless. The same guard-halted lead was 1 task on Zapier's Sub-Zap (3 if two Formatter steps bill), 4 credits on Make, 1 execution on n8n and 2 premium items on GoHighLevel. Any table that puts those four numbers in a column and invites comparison is misleading. Only cost per thousand leads is comparable, and only when computed on the day.

Two of the four can charge money you didn't plan to spend. Zapier moves you to a higher per-task rate when the plan's tasks run out. GoHighLevel has no free tier, and a trial converts to the full plan price unless it is cancelled. That is a real selection criterion for a client who will forget.

  3. Cost per 1,000 = the sum over outcomes of (meter units per run × count of that outcome in 1,000) × price per unit, at the tier the build requires, on the publication date.
  4. Convert to dollars from the vendor's own pricing page. State the date.

4.3 · The short-circuit finding

Make: the guard sits before the expensive branches, so a guard-halted lead costs 4 credits against 19 for a founder lead (measured). n8n: guard-halt and the full route are both 1 execution (measured). GoHighLevel: the short-circuit runs about 9 actions against about 15, but both bill 2 premium items, because the inbound trigger bills before the guard (derived). The same guard that saves 15 credits per lead on Make saves nothing on the meter here.

On a per-execution meter you order your steps for clarity; on a per-step meter you order them for cost. Those are not always the same order.

4.4 · Resilience is the part of the build that has a meter on it

On a per-step meter, each guard, error handler and alert module is billed on every run it executes. Make's measured numbers show it: a founder run with a live SMS attempt billed 19 operations, with the trigger and error-handler modules counted, against 1 execution for the whole of N1's route.

The same arithmetic explains, without being uncharitable about anyone, why published automation work tends to be happy-path: resilience is the part of the build that adds recurring line items and produces nothing visible when it works. That is the honest answer to a client who asks why the quote includes work they can't see.

It also reframes the n8n case. The better argument for self-hosting is not cost at volume but that per-execution metering removes the financial disincentive to build properly. That argument is measured for n8n and for Make's cost of guards; it is not a claim that the other platforms cannot be built resiliently.

4.5 · What is not in any of these numbers

  • Model tokens. Billed by the provider and not included.
  • Enrichment API calls. The free tier used on n8n returned 429 on roughly 1 in 3 calls.
  • SMS. A paid SMS provider was used on n8n and Make; none exists on Zapier and GoHighLevel as built (A2P 10DLC unregistered).
  • The VPS split and the operator's attention. 100% of the VPS is attributed to N1 until other builds run on it, and the GoHighLevel telemetry sink is hosted on that same VPS.
  • Build time, which is in §5 and which dominates the above for the first year.
  • The cost of being wrong. Migration between these platforms is manual. GoHighLevel documents snapshots shared by link or invite and no per-workflow file export (found 2026-10-10), and Zapier's JSON export is limited to Team and Enterprise plans, which this build did not use.

The two trade-offs to state plainly

Zapier's export gap is measured on the plan used, and it is worse than its retry gap. On the Free and Professional plans a Zap exists only inside Zapier and the migration path is a person rebuilding it by reading it; Zapier lists JSON export of Zaps on Team and Enterprise plans, which were not tested. For a client whose operations depend on it, that is a more consequential property than any per-task price.

n8n's ownership advantage is also its liability. The same sentence, you own the server, is the reason to choose it and the reason not to. The recommendation against n8n for a client with nobody to run it is argued, not tested, but the $5.35 invoice and the 59,260-byte export show what the ownership side of the trade looks like.

6 · FindingsEach is tagged: measured, derived, or reworded from the original specification because the captures narrowed it. If only three survive, they are 1, 3 and 4.

  1. The meter matters more than the price. Measured. The same guard-halted input was 1 task, 4 credits, 1 execution and 2 premium items. A client comparing headline prices is comparing four different products.
  2. Per-execution metering removes the cost of building properly. Measured. On n8n the guard-halt and the full route are both one execution (#168 against #186); on Make the same guard measures 4 against 19 credits.
  3. A downstream failure did not surface as a failed top-level run on any platform where probe 3 ran. Measured. n8n before its fix: Succeeded, no alert, logged as routed. Make: status SUCCESS, with an alert only because an error handler was hand-built. GoHighLevel: the parent reports the handoff executed, the child shows Failed, no alert. Only n8n after the added assert, and Make because someone built the alert, produce a visible signal.
  4. Step ceilings and plan limits changed the finished shape of two builds. Measured. Zapier's 32-step cap removed SMS and enrichment and forced three Zaps. GoHighLevel's SMS was removed for A2P cost. Only n8n and Make shipped an SMS path to probe.
  5. GoHighLevel's short-circuit saves actions but no meter cost. Derived. About 9 against about 15 executed actions, 2 premium items on both: the inbound trigger bills before the guard.
  6. GoHighLevel did not accept the nested input contract. Measured. Nested answers[] was not unwrapped, runs were contactless until Create/Update Contact was added, and the spec rule applies: this is a failed part of the test.
  7. Hold precision differs. Measured, one run each. n8n's Wait resumed at 09:00:00.000 exactly; GoHighLevel released 4 min 26 s after its 08:00:00 schedule; Make queued a send time 9 h wrong (a bug).
  8. The platform's own documentation can disagree with its meter. Measured. A Zapier Sub-Zap billed +1 task against a documented 2 (unresolved). GoHighLevel's wallet ledger logged 45 premium items between 1 and 6 Oct 2026 (39 inbound webhook, 5 custom webhook, 1 Slack message), every one at $0 under a "Free Tier" label on the trial; the free tier's size is not shown.
  9. The measured first-touch evidence does not rank GoHighLevel first. Measured. n8n about 10.4 s excluding the hold; Make 2.3 to 2.6 s for the whole run; Zapier not measured; GoHighLevel measured only after hours (6 h designed hold plus about 4 min 28 s scheduler lag). No two of the four are comparable, so the original "fastest first touch" claim is dropped.
  10. Instrumentation cost leaks into the benchmark. G1's second premium item is the telemetry webhook, and first touch has to be read from the execution log rather than the telemetry row.
  11. Zapier buys breadth, and pays in exit and, as far as it was probed, resilience. Reworded. The export gap is measured on the plan used. The resilience claim rests on no retry being surfaced, with three of five probes not applicable. Speed was not measured and is dropped.
  12. Make's failure handling is the best observed of the hosted three, with limits. Reworded. Observed: per-module error handlers, Resume, Slack alerts, and a save-time guard on connections. Not observed: any retry or backoff. Also observed: an unhandled error silently deactivated the scenario.
  13. Owning the platform is a different decision from owning the maintenance. Argued, with a measured $5.35 and export. "Cheapest" depends on stated volume: at 100 executions a month n8n is $53.50 per 1,000.
  14. GoHighLevel can charge you by accident, and that is the part of the original finding the evidence keeps. Reworded. Supported by the no-free-tier plan and the trial conversion; "fewest moving parts" is not supported by the captures.

8 · The finding that only an ecosystem produces

The client these four builds were written for sells software to a second kind of business: freight brokerages and 3PLs. They are in the same industry, adjacent in the same supply chain, and they need different automation stacks, and the reason is structural.

The software vendor's leads are digital-first and its automation's centre of gravity is data. A trial signup is a database row before it is a person. The work is enrichment, scoring, deduplication against an existing account table, provisioning into a product tenant, and keeping entitlements aligned with billing. Volume is high, per-lead urgency is moderate, and almost every system involved is one the company already owns and wants to keep owning. That points at Make or n8n.

The brokerage's leads are conversation-first and its automation's centre of gravity is the channel. A quote request is a phone call from a dispatcher who will call the next broker in ninety seconds if nobody picks up. The work is answering, capturing, routing to whoever covers that lane, and escalating when the SLA burns down. Volume is lower, per-lead urgency is extreme, and the systems involved are the phone, the text message and the calendar. That points at GoHighLevel.

The software vendor and its own customer needed different systems because one company's automation lives in its database and the other's lives in its phone. Everything else, including the cost comparison above, follows from that.

9 · What would change these recommendationsEach of these, if it moved, would change a recommendation.

  • Zapier ships native retry with backoff (re-check on the day). Finding 11 weakens and the solo-operator recommendation gets stronger.
  • Make's free-tier scenario ceiling moves. Changes how it's evaluated, not how it performs.
  • GoHighLevel ships a per-workflow export path (none was found in this build). The lock-in objection largely dissolves and three rows of the matrix get easier to recommend.
  • n8n Cloud pricing moves. Cloud Starter listed at €20/month for 2,500 executions on 2026-10-05; a cheaper or free tier would weaken the maintenance objection.
  • Volume assumptions change. Every blended number would be a function of a lead mix, and none was measured. The formula in §4.1 lets a client re-run it with their own mix.
One limitation, stated plainly and not buried. This compares four implementations built by one person over four weeks, learning three of the four platforms at production depth for the first time in that period. Build times reflect that. A Make specialist would build the Make version faster. What this comparison does have that vendor-neutral listicles don't is that the same person built all four, to the same specification, and broke all four the same way.
